# Christmas tree, fireplace and warm indoor lighting

Holiday family photos introduce three lighting traits that daytime outdoor snapshots almost never share:

1. **Very warm color temperature (2200 K – 2800 K)** from string lights, candles and table lamps.
2. **Multiple tiny point sources** (hundreds of LEDs on the tree) creating warm rim light on hair and knitwear shoulders, plus soft specular catchlights in the eyes.
3. **Mixed light when shot before dark** — cool winter window light (5500 K – 6500 K) on one side of the room and warm tungsten/tree light on the other.

---

## 1. Quick pre-check before combining two photos for a Christmas card

| What is in Photo 1 (the group photo) | Best Photo 2 of the missing person | Fix needed during compositing |
|---|---|---|
| Evening living room, tree + lamp light only | Indoor evening photo, or soft overcast daylight | Warm midtones/highlights (+15 to +30 Amber) and soften contrast |
| Daytime living room next to a window + lit tree | Window-lit indoor portrait facing the same side | Match cool key side + add subtle warm rim on the tree-facing shoulder |
| Outdoor snowy pine mini-session | Overcast outdoor photo (no hard noon sun) | Match cool shadow tint and add winter coat/knitwear harmony |

---

## 2. Matching knitwear texture and shadow falloff

- **Soft shadow floor contact**: Tree lights act like a tall, diffused vertical light bank rather than a single spotlight. Floor and sofa shadows should be broad and soft, falling *away* from the tree and primary lamp.
- **Seasonal wardrobe continuity**: If the rest of the family wears cable-knit sweaters or flannel and Photo 2 was taken in summer, either re-dress the added person in matching knitwear with an edit model or avoid placing them in the center foreground where fabric mismatch draws the eye first.
- **Avoid clipping the red channel**: Warm tungsten rooms already push red skin saturation high; warm the highlights gently rather than cranking global temperature until faces turn orange.
